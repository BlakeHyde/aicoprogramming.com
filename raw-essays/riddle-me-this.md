In November 2026 there was discourse online around an interview with Thomas Nagel, a philosopher of consciousness, and his opinion about AI as expressed in an interview with _Wired_:[^interview]

[^interview]: https://www.wired.com/story/just-ridiculous-a-famed-philosopher-takes-on-ai-consciousness/

> Interviewer: But isn't all consciousness subjective? Just like we don't know what it's like to be a bat, we don't know what it feels like to be a large language model.
>
> Nagel: Bats are conscious. We just don't have the imaginative capacity to form a conception of the aspects of that subjectivity that depend on echolocation. It would be radically unscientific to conclude that bats are not conscious from the fact that we can't imagine what it's like, but in the case of AI, there is a very simple reason that the supposition that they might be conscious is ridiculous. All they produce is language, and language by itself is not a natural manifestation of consciousness. I apologize for being too exercised about this.
>
> Interviewer: I welcome your passion.
>
> Nagel: This is a very distressing situation to me.

This argument is brief and sketchy, not a full-length or technical treatment of the subject, but it strikes me as fundamentally true, even obvious, that machines capable only of responding to and generating language are not conscious. This intuition interests me: many of the other people interacting with the interview seem to view it as ridiculous, insulting, or even immoral.

I think this response is from over-attributing meaning to language generation because of a lack of exposure to language as a subject of thought. Most normally educated people encounter early philosophical arguments expressed _in_ language (the chair, the "Platonic ideal" of the chair) or, at best, undergraduate Friday night philosophy: "What if my _blue_ is your _red?_" The questions of semantic grounding are more technical and less accessible.

Here's a thought experiment that might help.

Imagine a secret society. This secret society has its own internal language, a language of which all external observers are ignorant. The only time it's observed is at the vestibule of their inner sanctum. The member entering the sanctum walks up to the door, speaks through a slit, receives a response, and passes through. For example:

> Visitor: _Go gom tam i._<br/>
> Doorman: _Ba tin go to go._<br/>
> Visitor: _O ga._[^language]

[^language]: This is a real language, transformed with a few rules. There isn't enough detail here to decipher it, though.

Imagine a perfect recorder, set up to capture each interaction. It records thousands of visits, successful and unsuccessful, and builds a grammar of the language. This grammar is used to construct a large language model.

The language model is prompted, in the language of the land, to provide a spy with a valid phrase to say, and it does: _ti ta ti gi gi tim ma?_ The doorman responds with _gi tim_. The spy responds with the next suggestion, _to a go ta!_ and the slit slams shut. The door does not open.

What happened?

When the language model is generating phrases, it's based on linguistic fragments that it does not _understand_. It has been provided the transcriptions of the events, but there isn't a large corpus as there is in any real language. Only these interactions at the door.

The human members of the society know the language, and express conscious thought in their selection of their first phrase and their response. They, as individuals, choose the topic to introduce and their response to the doorman's response.

The language model can generate a plausible phrase because the math can determine which syllables are most likely to occur in the next position, but it can't respond to novel situations with insight. It isn't expressing any form of consciousness or thought in its selection of syllables, whereas the humans clearly are---even if we, the observers, aren't able to decipher the meaning of those syllables.

The more confusing situation is if the language model succeeds. If we imagine that the language model generates a reasonable response and the spy is allowed past the door, we might imagine that demonstrates conscious thought in the society's language. We've built artificial intelligence!

If it turns out that the conversation with the doorman is purposefully limited to a subset of the language, though---discussing the weather, for example---then our artificial intelligence is immediately going to fail when the spy tries to carry on a conversation within the society.

That doesn't sink our idea of artificial intelligence as conscious, though. If the spy himself had memorized the recordings and generated these plausible phrases, we certainly wouldn't say that his failure to converse (and immediate liquidation) was evidence of a lack of consciousness. However, we base that on his existence as an individual. The unfortunate spy kissed his girlfriend that Wednesday morning, had an espresso, and proceeded to his assignment with thoughts of skiing that weekend.

Let's back up to the unlucky spy's "success" at gaining entrance to the chapterhouse in the first place. In our two hypothetical worlds, one with the spy gaining entrance and being killed and the other with his failure at the door, the LLM generated exactly the same plausible greeting and response. How could this be?

In the world where he failed and is turned away without further incident, it was because he drank a bit too much on Tuesday, felt badly on Wednesday, and didn't try to gain entrance until Thursday. In the world where he's allowed inside, he skipped the wine and went to the door on Wednesday.

> Spy: _Ti ta ti gi gi tim ma?_ [Is it Thursday?]<br/>
> Doorman: _Gi tim._ [Of course.]<br/>
> Spy: _To a go ta!_ [Liar!]

In other words, the conversation is always plausible within the rules of the language, just as intended, but it's plausible as a series of signifiers without reference to the signified. The LLM succeeds at the aspects of the society's language that form a [small world][small-world] but does not have access to its references to the _real_.

[small-world]: {{ "/essays/small-world" | url }}

Let's move beyond the spy and his single instance of the model, in the world where he gains entrance but disappears. Perhaps the model was in his wristwatch, and disappears along with him. _His_ society is powerful in its own right, has many spies, and can provide them all with wristwatch LLMs. As more of their spies gain entrance and disappear, the door begins to be an object of fear and dread for the human spies.

Do you think the wristwatches feel any fear as their successive bearers lie awake the night before their missions?
